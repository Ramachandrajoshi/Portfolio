"It needs to scale" is the most common non-functional requirement and the least useful one. It says
nothing about *what* grows, *how fast*, *what must stay true while it grows*, or *what we are willing
to pay*. This article is the method I use to turn that sentence into something a design can be
judged against.

<Callout type="important" title="The short version">
  Scale is a property of a **workload under constraints**, measured against **quality-attribute
  scenarios**. Name the dimension that grows, the resource it exhausts first, and the behaviour you
  require when it does.
</Callout>

## Start with context, not technology

Before any component diagram, three questions:

1. **Who is affected, and what hurts today?** A design without a stated problem cannot be evaluated.
2. **What would make the change worth it?** The outcome you would point to in six months.
3. **What cannot change?** Contracts, regulations, budgets, team skills, existing systems.

The third question does most of the work. In a multi-tenant modernisation I describe in
[a case study](/architecture/multi-tenant-platform/), one contractual promise — physically separated
customer data — eliminated half of the tenancy models before anyone discussed databases.

## Name the dimension that grows

"Users" is rarely the right unit. Useful dimensions are specific:

| Vague | Specific | Resource it usually exhausts |
| --- | --- | --- |
| More users | Concurrent sessions at peak | Connections, memory |
| More data | Rows written per second; total retained | Write throughput; storage, index size |
| More customers | Tenants, and the skew between them | Isolation, noisy-neighbour headroom |
| Bigger requests | Tokens of context per request | GPU memory (KV cache) |
| More devices | Messages per second after an outage | Ingestion burst capacity |

Each row points at a different bottleneck — which is why "scalable" designs for one dimension often
fail on another.

## Do the arithmetic before the benchmark

A ten-line estimate frequently finds the constraint before any load test. Two examples from the case
studies on this site:

- **Database connections.** 400 tenants × 8 instances × a pool of 10 = 32,000 *potential* connections,
  against a PostgreSQL budget in the hundreds. The design needs a pooler and shard placement long
  before it needs more CPU.
- **GPU memory.** An 8B-class model needs ~128 KiB of KV cache per token. On a 24 GB GPU with FP16
  weights, that leaves room for roughly five 8k-token conversations — so concurrency, not model speed,
  is the constraint.

<Callout type="assumption">
  Back-of-the-envelope numbers are **design assumptions**. Write them down as such, and let them define
  what the load test must measure — never quote them as results.
</Callout>

## Write scenarios, not adjectives

A quality-attribute scenario has a stimulus, an environment and a measurable response:

> *When one tenant generates 10× its normal request rate (stimulus) during business hours
> (environment), other tenants' p95 latency degrades by less than 10 % (response).*

That sentence can be tested. "The system should be scalable and resilient" cannot.

## Compare options against the same criteria

Every significant decision deserves at least two credible options, compared on the scenarios that
matter here — not on popularity.

## Record the decision — including what you gave up

An Architecture Decision Record captures context, decision, alternatives and consequences. The
negative consequences are the most valuable part: they are what the next engineer needs to know
before changing the design.

<ADR
  id="EX-ADR-010"
  title="Serve dashboard reads from read replicas"
  status="accepted"
  date="2026-10-14"
  context="Dashboard reads are 90 % of database load and grow with customers; writes are flat. The business accepts data that is a few seconds old on dashboards but not on the order screen."
  decision="Route dashboard queries to two read replicas through a read-only connection string; keep order screens on the primary."
  alternatives={[
    { option: "Application cache", summary: "Cache dashboard results in Redis.", rejectedBecause: "Invalidation across many filter combinations is error-prone; replicas meet the target with less code." }
  ]}
  consequences={{
    positive: ["Read capacity scales with replicas", "Primary protected from reporting load"],
    negative: ["Replication lag must be monitored and shown to users when large", "Developers must choose the right connection per query"]
  }}
  risks={["Lag spikes during bulk writes"]}
  followUps={["Alert on replica lag > 5 s", "Lint rule: dashboard repositories must use the read-only context"]}
/>

## Checklist

- What grows, in what unit, and how fast?
- Which resource runs out first — and what is the arithmetic?
- Which scenarios define "good enough", with numbers?
- Which options were compared, on which criteria?
- What did we give up, and where is that written down?
- How will we know, in production, that an assumption was wrong?
