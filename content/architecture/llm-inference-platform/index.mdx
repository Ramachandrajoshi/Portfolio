## 1. Context and business problem

An organisation wants LLM capabilities — summarisation, extraction, classification and tool-using
agents — but its data cannot leave its own infrastructure. The available hardware is one server with
**two 24 GB GPUs** connected over PCIe. Budget for more hardware is a year away.

The question is not "which model is best?" but **"what can this hardware serve, to how many users, at
what latency — and what happens when demand exceeds it?"**

## 2. Requirements and constraints

| Constraint | Consequence for the design |
| --- | --- |
| 2 × 24 GB GPUs, PCIe only | Memory, not compute, is the binding constraint; cross-GPU communication is expensive. |
| Data must stay on-premises | No hosted fallback; overload must be handled locally and explicitly. |
| Mixed workloads (interactive, batch, agents) | Priority classes with separate latency objectives and budgets. |
| Small platform team | Prefer a proven inference engine with continuous batching over custom kernels. |
| Quality matters for structured outputs | Quantisation must be justified by task-specific evaluation, not generic benchmarks. |

## 3. Functional and non-functional requirements

**Functional**

1. OpenAI-compatible chat and completion APIs with streaming.
2. Per-tenant quotas and rate limits in **tokens**, plus usage accounting.
3. Priority classes: interactive, agent, batch.
4. Versioned model artefacts with promotion based on evaluation reports.

## 4. Quality attributes and acceptance criteria

Latency for LLMs is two numbers, not one:

- **Time to first token (TTFT)** — dominated by queueing and **prefill**, which processes the whole
  prompt and is largely **compute-bound**.
- **Inter-token latency (ITL)** — dominated by **decode**, which generates one token per step for every
  sequence in the batch and is largely **memory-bandwidth-bound**.

They respond to different levers (prompt length versus batch size and weight precision), so they are
specified and measured separately, per priority class.

## 5. Architecture and system boundaries

- The **gateway** is the policy boundary: identity, quotas, token estimation, cancellation, usage.
- The **scheduler** is the capacity boundary: it owns the only accurate view of free KV memory per
  replica and decides admission and routing.
- The **model servers** are the compute boundary: each GPU runs an independent engine replica with
  continuous batching and a paged KV cache.
- The **registry and evaluation harness** form the release boundary: artefacts and the evidence that
  they are good enough travel together.

## 9. Trade-offs and rejected alternatives

- **Admission control rejects work** that a permissive system would accept and serve slowly. That is
  the point: a 429 with Retry-After is a better outcome than every user's latency doubling.
- **Replicas don't pool memory.** A single very long context must fit within one GPU's KV budget —
  accepted, and enforced as a context-length cap per class.
- **Quantisation adds a release dependency** on the evaluation pipeline — accepted, because otherwise
  quality regressions are discovered by users.
- Rejected: **tensor parallelism over PCIe** for a model that fits on one GPU (per-layer
  synchronisation on every token), and **free engine preemption** as the overload strategy.

## 10. Security and trust boundaries

- **No egress from the GPU node**; model artefacts are pulled from the internal registry and verified by checksum.
- **Prompt and completion content is not logged** by default; metrics and traces carry token counts
  and timings only. Debug logging, when enabled, is time-boxed, access-controlled and redacted.
- **Per-tenant keys and quotas** at the gateway; usage is attributable without storing content.
- **Agents are constrained**: tool allow-lists, step and token budgets, and validation of model output
  before any tool executes — the model is treated as an untrusted component.
- **Prompt injection** is assumed possible in any retrieved content; tools with side effects require
  explicit confirmation or run with least privilege.

## 12. Scalability and performance reasoning

<Callout type="assumption" title="Theoretical estimates — assumptions stated">
  Model: an 8B-class decoder with 32 layers, grouped-query attention with 8 KV heads of dimension 128.
  FP16 KV cache. ~2.5 GB per GPU reserved for the runtime, activations and workspace. Memory
  bandwidth ~1 TB/s per GPU. These inputs illustrate the method; substitute the real model
  configuration and measure.
</Callout>

**KV cache per token**

KV bytes per token = 2 (K and V) × layers × KV heads × head dim × bytes per element
= 2 × 32 × 8 × 128 × 2 B = **131,072 B ≈ 128 KiB per token**.

An 8k-token sequence therefore holds about **1.07 GB** of KV cache (8,192 × 131,072 B).

**What fits on one 24 GB GPU**

<Tabs labels={["FP16 weights", "4-bit weights", "4-bit weights + FP8 KV"]} title="KV capacity per GPU by precision">
<TabPanel>

- Weights ≈ 8B × 2 B ≈ 16 GB; runtime reserve ≈ 2.5 GB.
- KV budget ≈ 24 − 16 − 2.5 ≈ **5.5 GB ≈ 42k tokens** (5.5 × 10⁹ B ÷ 131,072 B).
- ≈ **5 sequences at 8k**, or ≈ 14 at 3k average.

</TabPanel>
<TabPanel>

- Weights ≈ 4.5–5 GB (4-bit plus scales); runtime reserve ≈ 2.5 GB.
- KV budget ≈ 24 − 5 − 2.5 ≈ **16.5 GB ≈ 126k tokens**.
- ≈ **15 sequences at 8k**, or ≈ 42 at 3k average.

</TabPanel>
<TabPanel>

- Same weights; KV elements stored in 1 byte → **64 KiB per token**.
- KV budget ≈ 16.5 GB ≈ **252k tokens** — double, at a long-context accuracy cost that must be
  evaluated separately.

</TabPanel>
</Tabs>

Across two GPUs with 4-bit weights, the theoretical ceiling is ≈ 84 concurrent sequences at 3k tokens —
comfortably above the 24-sequence capacity target, leaving headroom for fragmentation, longer agent
contexts and estimation error. The target is set well below the ceiling on purpose.

**Decode speed upper bound**

Each decode step reads the weights once for the whole batch (plus each sequence's KV cache). An upper
bound for one stream is memory bandwidth ÷ bytes read per token:

- FP16: ~1 TB/s ÷ 16 GB ≈ **≤ 60 tokens/s** for a single stream.
- 4-bit: ~1 TB/s ÷ 5 GB ≈ **≤ 200 tokens/s** for a single stream.

Batching amortises the weight reads across sequences, so aggregate throughput rises with batch size
until KV reads and compute become the limit — which is why continuous batching, not more replicas of
single streams, is the throughput lever. Real engines reach a fraction of these bounds; the bounds tell
you which resource you are fighting.

**Prefill cost**

Prefill costs roughly 2 × parameters FLOPs per prompt token: ≈ 16 GFLOP/token for 8B parameters. A
4k-token prompt is ≈ 64 TFLOP — tens to hundreds of milliseconds of GPU time, during which that GPU's
decode steps for other sequences stall unless the engine interleaves (chunked prefill). Long prompts
from agents are therefore a latency risk for everyone on the same replica.

## 13. Observability and operational considerations

- **Latency by class:** TTFT and inter-token latency percentiles per priority class, plus queue wait.
- **Capacity:** KV-cache utilisation, free blocks, preemptions, running vs waiting sequences.
- **Throughput:** prompt and generated tokens per second per replica; batch size distribution.
- **GPU:** memory used, utilisation, temperature and clock throttling.
- **Quality in production:** structured-output validity rate, tool-call parse failures, refusal rate —
  cheap proxies that catch regressions between evaluation runs.
- **Usage:** tokens per tenant and class, to inform the next hardware purchase with evidence.

## 14. Testing and validation strategy

| Question | Method |
| --- | --- |
| Do the capacity estimates hold? | Load test with recorded prompt/completion length distributions; find the concurrency where p95 TTFT or ITL breaches its target. |
| Is overload handled explicitly? | Step load beyond capacity; assert interactive latency within target, batch 429s with Retry-After, zero preemption storms. |
| Is the quantised model good enough? | Task suites (extraction F1, tool-call validity, long-context retrieval) vs FP16 baseline, per artefact. |
| Do long prompts hurt neighbours? | Mix 16k-token agent prompts into interactive traffic; measure ITL impact with and without chunked prefill. |
| Does cancellation free capacity? | Abort streams mid-generation; assert KV blocks are released within one scheduling interval. |

## 15. Outcomes and lessons learned

**Outcomes.** No benchmark results are claimed here. Success would be demonstrated by the load test
finding a sustainable concurrency at or above the capacity target with latency objectives met, and by
the quantised artefact passing every task-suite tolerance.

**Lessons:**

- *Size in tokens of KV cache, not in requests or "users".* It is the resource that actually runs out.
- *Separate prefill and decode in your head and your dashboards.* They have different bottlenecks.
- *Quantisation is a capacity decision with a quality bill.* Pay it with evidence, per task.
- *Make overload explicit.* Queues and 429s are a feature; preemption storms are a failure mode.
- *Topology follows the interconnect.* Over PCIe, independent replicas usually beat tensor parallelism
  for models that fit.
