## 1. Context and business problem

A manufacturer operates plants with equipment from many vendors and eras. Engineers want trend data
for root-cause analysis, operators want live state and trustworthy alarms, and maintenance wants
work orders raised from conditions rather than calendars. Today data is trapped in individual
machines and historians on site.

The networks are the hard part: plant uplinks drop for minutes to days, bandwidth is limited, and
equipment cannot be changed. The platform must treat **disconnection, duplication and reordering as
the normal case**, not as exceptions.

## 2. Requirements and constraints

| Constraint | Consequence for the design |
| --- | --- |
| Uplinks are unreliable; outages of hours happen. | Buffering and identity must live at the edge. |
| Equipment cannot be modified; protocols vary. | A gateway adapts OPC UA / Modbus to one northbound format. |
| Several customers (tenants) share the platform. | Tenant identity must be bound to device credentials, not payloads. |
| Analysts know SQL; the team operates PostgreSQL. | Prefer a SQL time-series store with relational joins. |
| Alarm fatigue is already a problem on site. | Alarm logic must be stateful, not threshold-per-sample. |

## 3. Functional and non-functional requirements

**Functional**

1. Collect readings from heterogeneous devices with source timestamps.
2. Show live values and trends from seconds to years.
3. Evaluate alarm rules, notify on-call staff, support acknowledge and shelve.
4. Raise maintenance work orders from alarm conditions.
5. Keep an immutable raw archive for audit and reprocessing.

## 4. Quality attributes and acceptance criteria

Completeness and freshness conflict during recovery: replaying a backlog as fast as possible delays
live data, while protecting live data slows recovery. The design makes that trade-off explicit with
a **per-site replay rate** and live-data priority, and measures both sides (freshness p95 during
replay, time-to-drain).

## 5. Architecture and system boundaries

Three boundaries organise the system:

- **The site boundary.** Everything south of the edge gateway is the plant's network; the gateway
  makes only outbound connections and owns buffering, normalisation and message identity.
- **The ingestion boundary.** Untrusted device traffic becomes trusted, validated, de-duplicated
  events in exactly one place.
- **The storage boundary.** Consumers read from a durable event stream, so storage, alarming and
  future consumers evolve independently and can be rebuilt by replay.

## 7. Important design decisions

<ADR id="IOT-ADR-001" />

The message identity is what makes "at-least-once" safe. A sketch of the envelope the gateway
publishes:

```json
{
  "id": "site-17/plc-3/temp.zone2#18733042",
  "tenant": "t-042",
  "tag": "temp.zone2",
  "value": 412.6,
  "unit": "degC",
  "sourceTime": "2026-09-28T10:15:03.250Z",
  "seq": 18733042,
  "quality": "good"
}
```

Topic structure binds identity to authorisation: `t/{tenant}/s/{site}/d/{device}/telemetry`. The broker
ACL allows a certificate to publish only under its own tenant, site and device.

## 9. Trade-offs and rejected alternatives

- **Late data versus compression.** TimescaleDB compresses older chunks; inserting into them is
  costlier. Late readings beyond the compression horizon go through a correction job rather than the
  hot path — accepted complexity in exchange for storage efficiency.
- **Stateful alarm engine versus simplicity.** State must be partitioned and recovered; accepted,
  because stateless thresholds are what caused alarm fatigue.
- **One event stream versus direct writes.** An extra component to operate; accepted, because replay
  and independent consumers are what make the system evolvable.
- Rejected: **alarming inside the database** (couples alarm latency to write batching), and
  **per-tenant broker clusters by default** (operational cost; kept as an option for tenants that need it).

## 10. Security and trust boundaries

- **Device identity:** per-device X.509 certificates, provisioned through a registry; mTLS to the broker.
- **Authorisation by topic:** the certificate's identity determines which tenant/site/device topics it
  may publish to; tenant ids inside payloads are ignored for authorisation.
- **Outbound-only edge:** gateways never accept inbound connections from the internet.
- **Supply chain:** gateway agents are signed; updates are staged per site with rollback.
- **Data at rest:** encrypted storage; tenant-scoped keys for the object-storage archive.
- **Least privilege:** the storage writer has insert-only rights; the API has read-only rights.

## 12. Scalability and performance reasoning

<Callout type="assumption" title="Design assumptions — not measurements">
  200 sites × 2,000 tags at 1 Hz; ~120 bytes per reading on the wire; one site replaying an 8-hour
  backlog at 5× its live rate. These inputs exist to size the design and to define the load test.
</Callout>

**Steady state:**

- 200 × 2,000 × 1 Hz = **400,000 readings/s** across the fleet.
- At ~120 bytes each, ≈ **48 MB/s** inbound before compression — ingestion is a throughput problem, so
  readings are batched per device (e.g. one MQTT message per device per second containing all changed
  tags), cutting message count by orders of magnitude.

**Recovery:**

- One site's 8-hour backlog: 2,000 tags × 28,800 s ≈ **57.6 M readings**.
- Replaying at 5× its live rate (10,000 readings/s) drains in ≈ **96 minutes** — so freshness during
  replay depends on live-data priority, not on the backlog finishing quickly.

**Storage:**

- 400k readings/s ≈ 34.6 billion rows/day raw. Raw data is therefore kept briefly and compressed;
  dashboards read **continuous aggregates** (1-minute: 1/60 of the rows; 1-hour: 1/3,600).

These numbers say what to test, and where the design is most sensitive: messages per second at the
broker (reduced by batching) and rows per second at the database (reduced by COPY batches and
aggregates).

## 13. Observability and operational considerations

- **Edge health:** buffer depth, oldest unsent reading, reconnect count and clock drift per gateway.
- **Pipeline health:** consumer lag per group, rejected/quarantined message rate by reason, duplicate
  rate (a rising duplicate rate is an early sign of flapping connectivity).
- **Data health:** per-site completeness (expected vs received readings per hour), stale-tag detection.
- **Alarm health:** alarms per operator per hour, flapping and chattering alarms, standing alarms.
- **Storage health:** compression ratio, aggregate refresh lag, chunk count.

## 14. Testing and validation strategy

| Risk | Test |
| --- | --- |
| Loss or duplication on reconnect | Network-partition test with a gateway simulator: drop the uplink for N hours, reconnect, assert exactly-once storage by identity and per-hour completeness. |
| Ingestion capacity | Load test at the design load plus one replaying site; verify the lag-recovery criterion. |
| Alarm behaviour | Property-based tests on the rule engine with noisy synthetic signals; assert one notification per episode. |
| Late-data correctness | Inject readings older than the compression horizon; verify the correction path and aggregate refresh. |
| Tenant isolation | Cross-tenant publish attempts with valid certificates for another tenant; verify ACL rejection and audit. |
| Gateway resilience | Kill the agent mid-write; verify the disk queue recovers without corruption. |

## 15. Outcomes and lessons learned

**Outcomes.** As an illustrative design, it reports no production results. Success would be shown by
the partition test (no loss, no duplicates), the lag-recovery criterion under the design load, and
alarm-rate metrics within agreed limits after rollout.

**Lessons that generalise:**

- *Put identity where the data is born.* De-duplication is only possible if the producer stamps a
  stable identity before the first network hop.
- *Treat recovery as a workload.* Backlog replay is a predictable load spike; design and test for it.
- *Alarms are a product, not a query.* Operators judge the whole platform by alarm quality.
- *Aggregate early, keep raw cheaply.* Dashboards should never scan raw telemetry.
